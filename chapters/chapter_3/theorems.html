Calculus Made Easy

Geometrical, Algebraic, and Proof Explanations for Core Calculus Theorems

1. Rolle's Theorem

Statement

If a real-valued function $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$, differentiable on the open interval $(x, y)$—or $(a, b)$—and $f(a) = f(b)$, then there exists at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that:

f'(c) = 0
Geometrical Explanation

Imagine walking along a smooth, continuous mountain path starting at horizontal position $x = a$ and ending at $x = b$. If your starting elevation equals your ending elevation ($f(a) = f(b)$), and the path has no sharp corners (differentiable), you must have reached at least one absolute peak or valley where the trail temporarily flattens out completely horizontal. At that peak/valley, the slope of the tangent line is zero ($f'(c) = 0$).

Algebraic Explanation

Since $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval $[a, b]$, by the Extreme Value Theorem, $f$ must attain an absolute maximum $M$ and an absolute minimum $m$ on $[a, b]$. There are two cases:

  • Case 1: $M = m$. This means $f(x)$ is a constant function everywhere, so $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x$, meaning $f'(c) = 0$ is trivially true for any $c$.
  • Case 2: $M \neq m$. Since $f(a) = f(b)$, at least one of these extreme values must occur at an internal point $c \in (a, b)$. By Fermat's Theorem on stationary points, the derivative at a local extremum inside the domain must be zero. Therefore, $f'(c) = 0$.
Proof Outline:
  1. Invoke Extreme Value Theorem: $f$ achieves bounds on $[a, b]$.
  2. If bounds happen at endpoints where $f(a) = f(b)$, function is constant $\implies f'(c) = 0$.
  3. If extremum occurs inside $(a, b)$ at point $c$, let's look at the limit definition of derivative from left and right. Since it's a maximum/minimum, one sided limits force $f'(c) \ge 0$ and $f'(c) \le 0$, forcing $f'(c) = 0$. Q.E.D.

2. Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

Statement

If a function $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then there exists at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that:

f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}
Geometrical Explanation

The term $\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$ represents the slope of the secant line connecting the endpoints $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. The derivative $f'(c)$ represents the slope of the tangent line at point $c$. MVT states that somewhere between $a$ and $b$, the curve's instantaneous tangent line runs completely parallel to the secant line connecting the two ends.

Algebraic Explanation

MVT is a tilted/tilted generalization of Rolle's Theorem. We construct a new auxiliary function $g(x)$ that subtracts the linear secant line from our original function $f(x)$, forcing the endpoints to match zero:

g(x) = f(x) - \left[ f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a) \right]

Notice that $g(a) = 0$ and $g(b) = 0$. Because $g(x)$ satisfies all criteria of Rolle's Theorem, there exists a $c \in (a, b)$ such that $g'(c) = 0$, which cleanly yields the MVT formula when differentiated.

Proof Outline:
  1. Define auxiliary function $g(x) = f(x) - mx$, where $m$ is the secant slope $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$.
  2. Verify $g(x)$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.
  3. Check boundary values: $g(a) = g(b)$.
  4. Apply Rolle's Theorem to $g(x)$ to get $g'(c) = 0$.
  5. Since $g'(x) = f'(x) - m$, evaluating at $c$ gives $f'(c) = m$. Done!

3. Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem (CMVT)

Statement

If functions $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $g'(x) \neq 0$ for any $x$ in $(a, b)$, then there exists at least one number $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that:

\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)}
Geometrical Explanation

Think of a parametric curve defined by coordinates $(x(t), y(t)) = (g(t), f(t))$ for $t \in [a, b]$. The expression $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$ is the slope of the straight chord connecting the start and end points of this parametric path. Cauchy's theorem guarantees there is a point $t = c$ where the instantaneous slope vector or tangent slope of the curve ($\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$) equals the average slope of the chord.

Algebraic Explanation

Standard MVT deals with a single function against a linear baseline ($g(x) = x$). CMVT extends this concept to compare the relative rates of change of two distinct functions ($f$ and $g$). Notice that if you set $g(x) = x$, Cauchy's MVT collapses directly back into Lagrange's standard MVT.

Proof Outline:
  1. Construct an auxiliary function combining both functions:
    $h(x) = f(x) - k \cdot g(x)$ where $k = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$.
  2. Verify $h(a) = h(b)$.
  3. Apply Rolle's Theorem to $h(x)$, finding a point $c \in (a, b)$ where $h'(c) = 0$.
  4. Differentiate: $h'(c) = f'(c) - k \cdot g'(c) = 0$, leading straight to the target ratio.
