Planar vs. Spherical Geometry

On the unit sphere $S^2$, "straight lines" become great circles — intersections of the sphere with planes through its centre. This single change overturns familiar Euclidean facts.

Euclidean plane
  • Angle sum of a triangle $=180°$
  • Parallel lines exist (never meet)
  • Similar triangles of any size exist
  • Area is independent of angles
Spherical surface
  • Angle sum $> 180°$ always (spherical excess)
  • Any two great circles intersect — no parallels
  • Triangle shape fixes its size (area ∝ excess)
  • Area is determined by the angles

Core formulas

Law of Sines
\[ \frac{\sin a}{\sin A} = \frac{\sin b}{\sin B} = \frac{\sin c}{\sin C} \]

Ratio of the sine of a side to the sine of its opposite angle is constant for a given triangle.

Cosine Rule — Sides
\[ \cos a = \cos b \cos c + \sin b \sin c \cos A \]

Finds a side from the other two sides and their included angle (SAS).

Cosine Rule — Angles (dual/polar form)
\[ \cos A = -\cos B \cos C + \sin B \sin C \cos a \]

The polar-triangle dual of the side rule; finds an angle from the other two angles and their included side (ASA).

Girard's Theorem — Area & Excess
\[ \text{Area} = R^2(A+B+C-\pi) = R^2 E \]

The spherical excess $E$ measures how far the angle sum exceeds $\pi$; area grows linearly with it.

Napier's Rules ($C=90°$)
\[ \sin a = \sin c \sin A, \qquad \tan a = \tan c \cos B, \qquad \cos c = \cos a \cos b \]

Memorised via Napier's circular diagram of five parts for right-angled spherical triangles.

Polar Triangle Duality
\[ A' = \pi - a,\quad B'=\pi-b,\quad C'=\pi-c,\qquad a'=\pi-A,\; b'=\pi-B,\; c'=\pi-C \]

Each vertex of the polar triangle $\triangle A'B'C'$ is the pole of the opposite side of $\triangle ABC$.
