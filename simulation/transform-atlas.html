CH.1 — Continuous Fourier Transform

Winding a signal around a circle

The Fourier transform asks: if I wind this signal around a circle at a given frequency, where does its center of mass land? Wind at the "wrong" frequency and the wound curve balances near the origin. Wind at exactly one of the signal's true frequencies, and the curve's center of mass swings away from zero — that's a peak in the spectrum. The 3D view below stacks that winding through time, like a spring; its shadow on the floor is the 2D winding you'd draw by hand.

Why it works

Multiplying by e−i2πft spins each sample by an angle proportional to time. At a matching frequency, the spinning cancels the signal's own oscillation, so samples pile up on one side instead of cancelling out.

What to try

Set the winding frequency slider to exactly f₁, then f₂. Watch the bright centroid marker jump away from the central axis — and watch the matching peak light up in the spectrum below.

Where it's used

Audio EQ, image compression (JPEG), spectral analysis, communications — anywhere "what frequencies make up this signal" is the question.

CH.2 — Laplace Transform

A landscape over complex frequency

Laplace generalizes Fourier by letting frequency go complex: s = σ + jω. The extra real axis σ captures growth or decay, not just oscillation. For a simple system with one pole, |H(s)| forms a mountain that spikes to infinity exactly at the pole — drag the pole into the left half-plane and the system is stable; drag it right, and it isn't.

Why it works

Adding the real part σ to the exponent lets the transform "reach" signals that grow or decay — including ones a plain Fourier integral can't handle, by tilting the integration until it converges.

What to try

Push σₚ positive: the mountain peak crosses into the right half-plane and the impulse response below explodes — a hallmark of an unstable system.

Where it's used

Control systems, circuit analysis, stability design — anywhere you need to know whether a system settles down or blows up.

CH.3 — Hankel Transform (order 0)

Fourier's circularly symmetric cousin

When a 2D function only depends on radius r — a lens's point-spread function, a circular aperture, a drumhead's vibration — its 2D Fourier transform collapses to a 1D integral against a Bessel function J₀ instead of a complex exponential. That's the Hankel transform. The ripple you're steering below is a real radially symmetric surface; the chart is its actual computed radial spectrum.

Numerically integrated ∫f(r)J₀(kr)r dr over the visible surface. A tighter envelope (small σ) always spreads the spectrum wider — the radial uncertainty principle.
Why it works

Expanding a 2D Fourier transform in polar coordinates and integrating out the angle leaves exactly one function of angle behind: the Bessel function J₀, which plays the role that e−iωt plays in 1D.

What to try

Shrink σ to squeeze the bump tighter — the radial spectrum widens. Raise k₀ to add ripples — the spectrum's energy shifts outward to that ring of spatial frequency.

Where it's used

Optics and lens design, antenna beam patterns, X-ray diffraction, and any imaging system built around circular symmetry.

CH.4 — Z-Transform

Laplace's discrete-time twin

For sampled, discrete signals, the Z-transform plays Laplace's role: z = rejθ replaces continuous complex frequency with a complex number on a plane. Setting r = 1 — walking around the unit circle — recovers the discrete-time Fourier transform. A pole's distance from that circle tells you everything about whether the discrete system is stable.

The stem plot below is the actual inverse Z-transform of the pole you've placed. Push |a| past 1 and it diverges instead of decaying.
Why it works

Writing a discrete sequence as coefficients of powers of z−1 turns convolution — the core operation of digital filters — into ordinary multiplication of polynomials.

What to try

Slide the pole outside the unit circle (|a| > 1) and watch the stem plot's decaying staircase turn into an exploding one — instability made visible.

Where it's used

Digital filter design, audio DSP, control of sampled systems — anywhere a computer processes a signal one sample at a time.
