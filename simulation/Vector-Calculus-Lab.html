The Gradient \(\nabla f\)

The gradient transforms a scalar field into a vector field pointing in the direction of steepest ascent.

1 Conceptualization & Definition

The gradient is the first fundamental vector operator. It acts on a scalar field — a function that assigns a single real number to every point in space — and produces a vector field.

📘 Formal Definition

Given a differentiable scalar field \(f:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}\), the gradient of \(f\) is the vector field defined by

\[\nabla f(x,y,z) = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right\rangle.\]

In compact notation, this is the application of the del operator to the scalar function \(f\):

\[\nabla f = \left(\mathbf{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \mathbf{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \mathbf{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)f.\]

In two dimensions, the gradient reduces to \(\nabla f(x,y) = \langle f_x,\, f_y\rangle\). The gradient is sometimes denoted \(\text{grad}\, f\), a notation still common in physics and engineering.

2 Geometric Interpretation

🧭 Physical Meaning

At each point, the gradient vector \(\nabla f\) satisfies three fundamental geometric properties:

  1. Direction: It points in the direction of maximum increase of \(f\) (steepest ascent).
  2. Magnitude: \(\|\nabla f\|\) equals the maximum rate of change (the maximum directional derivative).
  3. Orthogonality: \(\nabla f\) is everywhere perpendicular to the level surface \(f(x,y,z)=C\) passing through that point.

Level Surfaces and Contour Lines

A level surface \(S = \{(x,y,z) : f(x,y,z) = C\}\) is a surface on which the function is constant. In two dimensions these reduce to level curves or contours. Topographical maps use contours to represent elevation — a scalar field — and the gradient always points directly uphill, perpendicular to the contour lines.

3 Mathematical Derivation

🧮 Deriving the Directional Derivative

Let \(f(x,y)\) be differentiable and \(\mathbf{u}=\langle u_1,u_2\rangle\) a unit vector. The directional derivative of \(f\) in direction \(\mathbf{u}\) at \((x,y)\) is defined by

\[D_{\mathbf{u}}f(x,y) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x+hu_1,\, y+hu_2) - f(x,y)}{h}.\]

Define \(g(h) = f(x+hu_1,\, y+hu_2)\). By the multivariable chain rule:

\[g'(h) = f_x(x+hu_1,\, y+hu_2)\cdot u_1 + f_y(x+hu_1,\, y+hu_2)\cdot u_2.\]

Since \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(x,y) = g'(0)\), we obtain

\[D_{\mathbf{u}}f = f_x u_1 + f_y u_2 = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}.\]
🧮 Proof: Gradient Points in the Direction of Maximum Increase

Using the geometric definition of the dot product with \(\|\mathbf{u}\|=1\):

\[D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\|\,\|\mathbf{u}\|\cos\theta = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta,\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\nabla f\) and \(\mathbf{u}\). This expression is:

  • Maximized when \(\cos\theta = 1\) (i.e., \(\theta=0\)), giving \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \|\nabla f\|\). So \(\mathbf{u}\) points along \(\nabla f\).
  • Minimized when \(\cos\theta = -1\) (i.e., \(\theta=\pi\)), giving \(-\|\nabla f\|\). So steepest descent is \(-\nabla f\).
  • Zero when \(\cos\theta = 0\) (i.e., \(\theta = \pi/2\)). So movement perpendicular to \(\nabla f\) produces no change in \(f\).
🧮 Proof: Gradient is Normal to Level Surfaces

Let \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) be any smooth curve lying on the level surface \(S = \{f(x,y,z) = C\}\). Since \(f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = C\) is constant, differentiating with respect to \(t\) and applying the chain rule gives:

\[\frac{d}{dt} f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \nabla f(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = 0.\]

Since \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) is a tangent vector to \(S\) and this holds for every curve on \(S\), the gradient is orthogonal to the entire tangent plane at \(\mathbf{r}(t)\). Hence \(\nabla f\) is the surface normal.

🔗 Connection to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for Line Integrals

If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\) (i.e., \(\mathbf{F}\) is a gradient field), then for any curve \(C\) from \(A\) to \(B\):

\[\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(B) - f(A).\]

This is the Gradient Theorem — the line integral depends only on the endpoints. Such fields are called conservative; physical examples include gravitational and electrostatic fields.

4 Worked Example

📐 Problem: Compute the Gradient in 3D

Given the scalar field \(f(x,y,z) = x^2 y + y z^2\), compute \(\nabla f\) and evaluate it at the point \(P_0 = (1, 2, -1)\). Interpret the result geometrically.

Step 1 — Compute Partial Derivatives

\[ \begin{aligned} f_x &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 y + y z^2) = 2xy,\\[6pt] f_y &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 y + y z^2) = x^2 + z^2,\\[6pt] f_z &= \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(x^2 y + y z^2) = 2yz. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, the gradient vector field is:

\[\nabla f(x,y,z) = \langle 2xy,\; x^2+z^2,\; 2yz\rangle.\]

Step 2 — Evaluate at \(P_0 = (1,2,-1)\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla f(1,2,-1) &= \langle 2(1)(2),\; 1^2+(-1)^2,\; 2(2)(-1)\rangle \\ &= \langle 4,\; 2,\; -4\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

Step 3 — Magnitude (Maximum Rate of Increase)

\[\|\nabla f(P_0)\| = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{16+4+16} = \sqrt{36} = 6.\]

Step 4 — Geometric Interpretation

  • The vector \(\langle 4, 2, -4\rangle\) is the direction of steepest ascent at \(P_0\).
  • The function increases most rapidly at a rate of 6 units per unit distance in this direction.
  • The direction of steepest descent is \(-\nabla f = \langle -4, -2, 4\rangle\) with rate \(-6\).
  • The value \(f(P_0) = 1^2\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1)^2 = 4\), so the level surface through \(P_0\) is \(x^2y+yz^2=4\). The gradient \(\langle 4,2,-4\rangle\) is the normal to this surface at \(P_0\).
  • A unit vector in the direction of maximum increase is \(\mathbf{u} = \tfrac{1}{6}\langle 4,2,-4\rangle = \langle \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3}, -\tfrac{2}{3}\rangle\).
Operator 2 of 3 · Vector → Scalar

The Divergence \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\)

Divergence measures how much a vector field flows outward from (or inward toward) a point — a local measure of source strength.

1 Conceptualization & Definition

Unlike the gradient (which maps scalar → vector), divergence acts on a vector field and produces a scalar field. It quantifies the local "outflow density" of the field.

📘 Formal Definition

For a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P(x,y,z),\, Q(x,y,z),\, R(x,y,z)\rangle = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}\), the divergence is

\[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}.\]

In index notation this is simply \(\partial_i F_i\) (Einstein summation). Notice the notation: the del operator takes the formal dot product with the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\).

In two dimensions, \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle P,Q\rangle\) and \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = P_x + Q_y\). The divergence is sometimes written \(\text{div}\,\mathbf{F}\).

2 Geometric Interpretation

🧭 Physical Meaning

If \(\mathbf{F}\) represents the velocity field of a fluid, then at each point:

  • \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} > 0\): A source — more fluid is leaving than entering an infinitesimal volume.
  • \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} < 0\): A sink — fluid is being absorbed.
  • \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0\): The fluid is incompressible (solenoidal field); flux in equals flux out.

Equivalently, \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}(P)\) equals the net outward flux per unit volume in the limit of a vanishingly small region around \(P\).

3 Mathematical Derivation

🧮 Deriving Divergence from Flux

Consider an infinitesimal rectangular box \(V = [x,x+\Delta x]\times[y,y+\Delta y]\times[z,z+\Delta z]\) with volume \(\Delta V = \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\). The net outward flux through the boundary \(\partial V\) is \(\Phi = \iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\).

Flux through the two faces perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis. The outward normals are \(\pm\mathbf{i}\) and the areas are \(\Delta y\,\Delta z\):

\[\Phi_x \approx P(x+\Delta x,y,z)\Delta y\Delta z - P(x,y,z)\Delta y\Delta z.\]

Factoring and using the limit definition of the derivative:

\[\Phi_x \approx \frac{P(x+\Delta x)-P(x)}{\Delta x}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z \;\approx\; \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\,\Delta V.\]

Repeating for the \(y\)-faces and \(z\)-faces:

\[\Phi \approx \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\right)\Delta V.\]

Dividing by \(\Delta V\) and taking the limit \(\Delta V\to 0\):

\[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta V}\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{A}.\]
🔗 Gauss's Divergence Theorem

Integrating the local definition over a finite region \(\Omega\) with boundary \(\partial\Omega\) and outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) gives the Divergence Theorem:

\[\iiint_\Omega (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F})\,dV = \iint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dA.\]

The total outward flux through a closed surface equals the integral of the divergence throughout the enclosed volume. It is the 3D analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

4 Worked Example

📐 Problem: Compute Divergence and Total Flux

Given the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x^2 y,\, yz^2,\, zx^2\rangle\), compute \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\), evaluate it at \(P_0 = (1,1,1)\), and use the Divergence Theorem to compute the total outward flux through the unit cube \([0,1]^3\).

Step 1 — Compute Partial Derivatives

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 y) &= 2xy,\\[4pt] \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(yz^2) &= z^2,\\[4pt] \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(zx^2) &= x^2. \end{aligned} \]

Step 2 — Assemble the Divergence

\[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 2xy + z^2 + x^2.\]

Step 3 — Evaluate at \(P_0 = (1,1,1)\)

\[\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}(1,1,1) = 2(1)(1) + 1^2 + 1^2 = 2+1+1 = 4.\]

The positive value confirms that the point \(P_0\) acts as a source: fluid is being created at a rate of 4 units of flux per unit volume in its neighborhood.

Step 4 — Total Outward Flux Through the Unit Cube (via Gauss's Theorem)

\[ \begin{aligned} \Phi &= \iiint_{[0,1]^3} (2xy + z^2 + x^2)\,dV \\[4pt] &= \left(\int_0^1 2x\,dx\right)\!\left(\int_0^1 y\,dy\right)\!\left(\int_0^1 dz\right) + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} \\[4pt] &= (1)\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\!(1) + \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} = \frac{7}{6} \approx 1.167. \end{aligned} \]

Step 5 — Interpretation

  • Locally at \(P_0\): divergence \(= 4\) (strong source).
  • Globally over the unit cube: total net flux \(= 7/6 > 0\), so on average the cube acts as a source.
  • Directly computing the flux through each of the six faces (lengthy exercise) would produce the identical result — the beauty of Gauss's theorem is that it reduces a 6-surface flux computation to a single volume integral.

The Curl \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\)

Curl captures the local rotational tendency of a vector field — the density of circulation around an infinitesimal loop.

1 Conceptualization & Definition

The curl acts on a vector field and returns another vector field. It quantifies the local rotation or "vorticity" of the field.

📘 Formal Definition

For \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P, Q, R\rangle\), the curl is defined as the formal cross product of the del operator with \(\mathbf{F}\):

\[\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\[4pt] \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\\[6pt] P & Q & R \end{vmatrix}.\]

Expanding the determinant:

\[\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \left\langle \frac{\partial R}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z},\; \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial R}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right\rangle.\]

In 2D, \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q\rangle\) and the curl reduces to a single scalar component \((\nabla\times\mathbf{F})_z = Q_x - P_y\), which we often treat as a pseudoscalar indicating rotation in the \(xy\)-plane.

A vector field with \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\) everywhere is called irrotational; if it is also defined on a simply-connected domain, it is necessarily conservative (a gradient field).

2 Geometric Interpretation

🧭 Physical Meaning

Imagine placing an infinitesimal paddle wheel at a point \(P\) in the field \(\mathbf{F}\) (interpreted as a fluid velocity):

  • Direction of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}(P)\): the axis of maximum rotation (by the right-hand rule).
  • Magnitude \(\|\nabla\times\mathbf{F}(P)\|\): twice the angular velocity of the paddle wheel.
  • If \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\) at \(P\), the paddle wheel does not spin — it may translate, but no local rotation occurs.

Formally, the component of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\) in the direction of a unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) equals the circulation density around an infinitesimal loop perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\).

3 Mathematical Derivation

🧮 Deriving Curl from Circulation

Consider a small rectangle \(C\) in the \(xy\)-plane with corners \((x,y), (x+\Delta x, y), (x+\Delta x, y+\Delta y), (x, y+\Delta y)\), traversed counterclockwise. The circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) is \(\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\).

The bottom and top edges (horizontal, parallel to \(\mathbf{i}\)) contribute:

\[\Gamma_{\text{horiz}} \approx P(x,y)\Delta x - P(x,y+\Delta y)\Delta x \approx -\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\Delta x\,\Delta y.\]

The right and left edges (vertical, parallel to \(\mathbf{j}\)) contribute:

\[\Gamma_{\text{vert}} \approx Q(x+\Delta x,y)\Delta y - Q(x,y)\Delta y \approx \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\Delta x\,\Delta y.\]

Summing and dividing by the area \(\Delta A = \Delta x\,\Delta y\):

\[(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})_z = \lim_{\Delta A\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta A}\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}.\]

By cyclic permutation we obtain the \(x\)- and \(y\)-components.

🔗 Stokes' Theorem

Integrating the local definition over a surface \(S\) with boundary \(\partial S\) and unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) yields Stokes' Theorem:

\[\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dA = \oint_{\partial S}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}.\]

The total circulation around a closed loop equals the flux of the curl through any surface bounded by the loop. This generalizes the 2D Green's theorem to arbitrary surfaces in 3D.

4 Worked Example

📐 Problem: Compute Curl and Apply Stokes' Theorem

Given the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle y^2,\, x^2,\, z\rangle\), compute \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\), evaluate it at \(P_0 = (1,2,3)\), and verify Stokes' Theorem by comparing (i) the surface integral of the curl over the unit disk in the \(xy\)-plane and (ii) the line integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) around its boundary circle.

Step 1 — Compute the Component Partial Derivatives

For \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R\rangle = \langle y^2,\, x^2,\, z\rangle\):

\[ \begin{aligned} R_y - Q_z &= 0 - 0 = 0, \\ P_z - R_x &= 0 - 0 = 0, \\ Q_x - P_y &= 2x - 2y = 2(x-y). \end{aligned} \]

Step 2 — Assemble the Curl

\[\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle 0,\; 0,\; 2(x-y)\rangle.\]

The curl has only a \(z\)-component: all rotation is in the \(xy\)-plane.

Step 3 — Evaluate at \(P_0 = (1,2,3)\)

\[\nabla\times\mathbf{F}(1,2,3) = \langle 0,\, 0,\, 2(1-2)\rangle = \langle 0,\, 0,\, -2\rangle.\]

The negative \(z\)-component tells us a paddle wheel at \(P_0\) rotates clockwise (viewed from above) with angular speed \(\omega = \tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\| = 1\) rad/s.

Step 4 — Verify Stokes' Theorem on the Unit Disk

Let \(S\) be the unit disk \(x^2+y^2\le 1,\, z=0\) with normal \(\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{k}\), and let \(\partial S\) be the unit circle \(C\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t,\, \sin t,\, 0\rangle\), \(0\le t\le 2\pi\).

Surface integral of the curl:

\[ \begin{aligned} \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\,dA &= \iint_S 2(x-y)\,dA \\ &= 2\int_0^{2\pi}(\cos\theta - \sin\theta)\,d\theta \cdot \int_0^1 r^2\,dr = 2\cdot 0 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = 0. \end{aligned} \]

Line integral around the boundary (with \(d\mathbf{r} = \langle -\sin t,\, \cos t,\, 0\rangle\,dt\)):

\[ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} (-\sin^3 t + \cos^3 t)\,dt = 0, \]

since both \(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^3 t\,dt = 0\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^3 t\,dt = 0\) by symmetry.

Step 5 — Verification

\[\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dA \;=\; 0 \;=\; \oint_{\partial S}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}.\]
  • Stokes' theorem is verified.
  • Both the circulation and the surface flux of the curl vanish because \(\mathbf{F}=\langle y^2, x^2, 0\rangle\) produces clockwise rotation on one half-plane and counterclockwise on the other, perfectly canceling on the symmetric disk.

🎓 Key Identities to Remember

For any twice-differentiable scalar field \(f\) and vector field \(\mathbf{F}\):

\[\nabla\times(\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = 0.\]

These express the fundamental chain: gradient fields are irrotational and curl fields are incompressible. They are the vector-calculus analogues of \(d^2 = 0\) in exterior calculus.
