The Five
Perfect Solids

For over two thousand years, geometers have known there are exactly five convex regular polyhedra — bodies of such perfect symmetry that Plato bound them to fire, earth, air, water, and the cosmos itself.

§ 4.1 — Foundations

What makes a solid platonic

Before the five solids can be named, three ideas need pinning down: convexity, the polyhedron itself, and what it means for one to be perfectly regular.

4.1.1 — Convex Set

A subset X of Rⁿ is convex if, for every pair of points x, y in X, the segment joining them lies entirely within X — that is, for each t in [0,1], tx + (1−t)y remains in X. A convex set is closed under this kind of combination.

4.1.2 — Convex Polyhedron

A subset of R³ carved out by finitely many linear inequalities is a convex polyhedron — a solid built from finitely many planar faces. The intersection of two faces is an edge; the intersection of two edges is a vertex.

4.1.3 — Regular Polyhedron

A polyhedron is regular when every face, edge, and vertex is identical to every other — the same number of edges meets at each vertex, always at the same angles.

4.1.4 — Platonic Solid

A convex regular polyhedron is called a platonic solid. Remarkably, there are only five of them — each fully described by the shape of its face and how many faces meet at each vertex.

§ 4.1.6

Duality

Join the midpoints of adjacent faces of a platonic solid with new edges, and the shape you get back is its dual — again platonic. Do it twice, and the original solid returns, only smaller. The tetrahedron is self‑dual; the cube pairs with the octahedron; the dodecahedron pairs with the icosahedron.

Exercise — equal edges

Show that a platonic solid and its dual have the same number of edges.

Solution — Corresponding to each face of a platonic solid there is a vertex of its dual, so the solid and its dual interchange F and V. Euler's formula V − E + F = 2 is symmetric in V and F, so E must stay fixed.
