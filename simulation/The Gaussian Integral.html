The Gaussian Integral

Visualizing and Understanding ∫e⁻ˣ² dx = √π

Introduction

The Gaussian integral, also known as the Euler-Poisson integral, is the integral of the Gaussian function e⁻ˣ² over the entire real line. It is named after the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss.

Theorem

With the notation:

I = ∫-∞∞ e⁻¹⁄₂ˣ² dx, J = ∫0∞ e⁻ˣ² dx, and K = ∫-∞∞ e⁻ᴾᴵˣ² dx

We have:

I = √(2π), or equivalently J = √π/2, or equivalently K = 1

This integral is fundamental in probability theory, statistics, and quantum mechanics. Despite its simple appearance, it has no elementary antiderivative, which makes its evaluation particularly interesting.

Proofs of the Gaussian Integral

The Gaussian integral can be proven in many different ways. Here are some of the most elegant proofs:

1 Polar Coordinates

The most famous proof squares the integral and converts to polar coordinates:

J² = ∫∫ e⁻⁽ˣ²⁺ʸ²⁾ dxdy = ∫∫ e⁻ʳ² r drdθ = π/4

Thus J = √π/2. This method was popularized by Poisson.

2 Change of Variables

By substituting x = yt in the inner integral and applying Fubini's theorem:

J² = ∫∫ ye⁻ʸ²⁽ᵗ²⁺¹⁾ dtdy = ∫ dt/(2(t²+1)) = π/4

This proof is due to Laplace and historically precedes the polar coordinates method.

3 Differentiation Under Integral

Define A(t) = (∫₀ᵗ e⁻ˣ² dx)² and differentiate with respect to t:

A'(t) = -B'(t) where B(t) = ∫₀¹ e⁻ᵗ²⁽¹⁺ˣ²⁾/(1+x²) dx

This leads to A(t) = -B(t) + C, and taking limits gives J² = π/4.

4 Volume of Revolution

Revolve z = e⁻¹⁄₂ˣ² around the z-axis and compute the volume in two ways:

V = ∫₀¹ πr(z)² dz = 2π (horizontal slices)
V = ∫ A(x) dx = I² (vertical slices)

Equating gives I² = 2π, so I = √(2π).

5 Gamma Function

Using the Gamma function property:

Γ(1/2) = 2∫₀∞ e⁻ˣ² dx = 2J
Γ(1/2)² = π ⇒ 4J² = π ⇒ J = √π/2

This connects the Gaussian integral to the Gamma function.

6 Fourier Transform

Using properties of the Fourier transform:

If f(x) = e⁻ˣ²⁄² then (ℱf)(y) = Ce⁻ʸ²⁄²

Applying the Fourier transform twice gives 2πf(-x) = I²f(x), so I² = 2π.
