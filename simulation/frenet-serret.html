Part one

The moving frame

Three vectors, recomputed at every point, form a local coordinate system that travels with the curve — often called the TNB frame or the trihedron.

Direction of travel

Tangent, T

Points along the curve's direction of motion. It is simply the velocity vector, rescaled to unit length.

$$\mathbf{T}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\lVert \mathbf{r}'(t) \rVert}$$
Direction of turning

Normal, N

Points toward the center of the curve's turn — the direction T is instantaneously rotating into.

$$\mathbf{N}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{T}'(t)}{\lVert \mathbf{T}'(t) \rVert}$$
Completes the frame

Binormal, B

Perpendicular to the plane of T and N. Its rate of change measures how the curve twists out of that plane.

$$\mathbf{B}(t) = \mathbf{T}(t) \times \mathbf{N}(t)$$

Part two

Curvature & torsion

Two scalar functions of t quantify everything about the curve's shape: how sharply it bends, and how sharply it twists out of its own bending plane. Both plots track the marker as it moves.

● Curvature κ(t) — rate of bending

$$\kappa = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \rVert^{3}}$$

● Torsion τ(t) — rate of twisting

$$\tau = \frac{(\mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'') \cdot \mathbf{r}'''}{\lVert \mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{r}'' \rVert^{2}}$$

Part three

The Frenet–Serret formulas

The punchline: the derivatives of T, N and B — with respect to arc length s — are expressed entirely in terms of each other, weighted only by κ and τ.

$$ \frac{d}{ds}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}\\ \mathbf{N}\\ \mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \kappa & 0 \\ -\kappa & 0 & \tau \\ 0 & -\tau & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}\\ \mathbf{N}\\ \mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix} $$

$$\mathbf{T}' = \kappa \mathbf{N}$$

T changes purely toward N. How fast it turns is exactly the curvature κ — this is the definition of curvature made rigorous.

$$\mathbf{N}' = -\kappa \mathbf{T} + \tau \mathbf{B}$$

N's rotation has two parts: it falls back toward −T to keep the frame orthonormal, and swings toward B at a rate set by torsion τ.

$$\mathbf{B}' = -\tau \mathbf{N}$$

B tips toward −N. This is the twisting motion: a nonzero τ means the curve is leaving its instantaneous osculating plane.

▸ Why the matrix is skew-symmetric (short derivation)
Because T, N, B stay mutually orthonormal for every s, differentiating each dot-product identity forces structure on their derivatives.
$$\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{T}=1 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{T}'\cdot\mathbf{T}=0$$
so T′ has no component along T — it must live in the N–B plane.
Differentiating T·N = 0 and N·B = 0 and B·T = 0 in turn, and defining κ = T′·N and τ = N′·B, pins down every remaining coefficient. The result is forced to be skew-symmetric — that's what guarantees T, N, B stay an orthonormal frame at every point along the curve, not just at the start.
$$\mathbf{T}'=\kappa\mathbf{N},\qquad \mathbf{N}'=-\kappa\mathbf{T}+\tau\mathbf{B},\qquad \mathbf{B}'=-\tau\mathbf{N}$$
