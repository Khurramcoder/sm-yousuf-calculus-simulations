01 · Foundations

What makes a four-vector a four-vector

Not every list of four numbers qualifies. A four-vector is defined by how it behaves — it must transform between inertial frames exactly the way spacetime coordinates do.

In special relativity, space and time are stitched into a single four-dimensional manifold. A point — an event — is labeled by a four-position, and any quantity built from the same transformation rule is a four-vector:

A four-vector is an ordered set of four components, , that transforms under a change of inertial frame according to the Lorentz transformation — the same linear rule that relates one observer's coordinates of space and time to another's.

The essential payoff is invariance: from the components of any four-vector we can build a single number, the Minkowski inner product, and every inertial observer computes the same value for it — even though they disagree on the individual components.

This document uses the mostly-minus signature , common in particle physics; the mostly-plus convention just flips an overall sign and changes no physics.

02 · Canon

The four four-vectors

Almost every relativistic quantity you'll meet is built from one of these. Each takes the same skeleton — a time-like piece paired with an ordinary 3-vector — and each carries its own invariant.

I

Four-position timelike separation

Locates an event in spacetime: a time of occurrence bundled with where it happened. Its invariant is the interval between the event and the origin — the quantity every observer agrees measures spacetime "distance."

II

Four-velocity always timelike

The rate of change of four-position with respect to proper time — the time on the moving object's own clock, not the observer's. Its magnitude is always exactly c, whatever the object's ordinary speed.

III

Four-momentum mass-shell

Mass times four-velocity: energy and ordinary momentum unified. Its invariant is the rest mass — the one measure of "how much stuff" that every observer, in every frame, computes identically.

IV

Four-current source term

Charge density and ordinary current density combined into one object. It's what sources the electromagnetic field, and its conservation, ∂μJμ = 0, is charge conservation written covariantly.

03 · Why it matters

One quantity every observer agrees on

Rotate ordinary 3-vectors and their components change but their length doesn't. Boost a four-vector between inertial frames and the same thing happens — the "length" here is the spacetime interval.

Move the β slider in the simulation above and watch the vector's components swing wildly — ct and x trade off against each other under the boost — while the readout for s² never moves. That invariance is the entire point of building physics out of four-vectors: it lets us write laws that look the same to every inertial observer, because both sides of the equation are built from quantities that transform the same covariant way.

The sign of s² also splits every four-vector into three physical classes. A timelike separation (s² > 0) can be crossed by a slower-than-light signal — cause can reach effect. A spacelike separation (s² < 0) cannot; different observers can even disagree on which of two such events happened first. A null separation (s² = 0) is exactly what light itself traces out — the light cone rendered in the simulation.
