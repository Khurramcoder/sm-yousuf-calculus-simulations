05step 3 — the line integral, edge by edge

Same answer, walked around the rim in three legs:

Along the base, y = 0 and dy = 0, so every term vanishes.

Along the right edge, x = 2 is fixed and dx = 0.

Along the hypotenuse, y = 2x and dy = 2dx, integrated from x = 2 back to x = 0.

Watch the "line integral" counter during the trace: it sits at 0 on the base, jumps to 24 on the right edge, then falls back to 4 on the hypotenuse. The overshoot-and-settle is not a rounding artifact — it is the three partial sums 0, 24, −20 arriving one at a time.

06reading the 3D scene

base triangle, region D boundary C, counter-clockwise curl-height columns

The flat teal triangle is D sitting in the z = 0 plane. The amber loop is C. Each rose or pale column stands over a small patch of D with height Mx − Ly = 3 − x² — tall and warm where curl is strongly positive near x = 0, thin and pale as x approaches √3, and dipping below the base where curl turns negative past that point. Summing every column's signed volume gives the same 4 that the boundary trace gives.

Green's Theorem is the flat-earth version of Stokes' Theorem — restrict a surface integral of curl to a surface lying in a single plane, and the surface integral collapses to this double integral, while the boundary circulation becomes an ordinary 2D line integral. It is also why aerodynamicists can get circulation around an airfoil cross-section by integrating vorticity over the enclosed area instead of tracking the flow around the whole boundary.
