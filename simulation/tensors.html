Curvilinear Coordinates
A coordinate system for Euclidean space where coordinate lines may be curved. The position vector $\mathbf{r}$ is expressed as a function of generalized coordinates $(q^1, q^2, q^3)$.
$$ \mathbf{r} = x(q^1,q^2,q^3)\,\mathbf{e}_x + y(q^1,q^2,q^3)\,\mathbf{e}_y + z(q^1,q^2,q^3)\,\mathbf{e}_z $$
Surfaces $q^i = \text{const}$ are coordinate surfaces. Their pairwise intersections form coordinate curves. The tangent vectors to these curves define the natural basis.
$$ \mathbf{b}_i = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i} \quad\text{(natural / covariant basis)} $$
Common systems: Cartesian Polar Cylindrical Spherical
Unit Vectors & Lamé Coefficients
Natural basis vectors are tangent to coordinate curves but generally not unit length. The scale factors (Lamé coefficients) $h_i$ normalize them.
$$ h_i = \|\mathbf{b}_i\| = \left\|\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i}\right\|, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{b}_i}{h_i} $$
Polar: $h_r = 1,\; h_\theta = r$.
Spherical: $h_r = 1,\; h_\theta = r,\; h_\phi = r\sin\theta$.
$$ \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i \cdot \hat{\mathbf{e}}_j = \delta_{ij} \quad\text{(orthogonal systems)} $$
In the 3D view, the colored arrows show the local orthonormal basis at the current point. Drag the point or change coordinates to see them rotate.
Arc Length, Volume & Metric
The metric tensor $g_{ij}$ encodes distances and angles. For orthogonal systems it is diagonal with entries $h_i^2$.
$$ ds^2 = g_{ij}\,dq^i\,dq^j, \qquad dV = h_1 h_2 h_3\,dq^1 dq^2 dq^3 = \sqrt{g}\,dq^1 dq^2 dq^3 $$
The 3D view visualizes an infinitesimal volume element (blue wireframe box) whose edges scale with the local $h_i$.
Polar area element
$dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$
Spherical volume
$dV = r^2\sin\phi\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$
$$ g_{ij} = \mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i}\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^j} $$
Contravariant & Covariant Vectors
A vector $\mathbf{V}$ is geometrically invariant, but its components depend on the chosen basis. Contravariant components pair with covariant basis vectors and vice versa.
Contravariant
$\mathbf{V} = V^i \mathbf{b}_i$
Covariant
$\mathbf{V} = V_i \mathbf{b}^i$
$$ V^i = \mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{b}^i, \qquad V_i = \mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{b}_i $$
Key insight: The gradient $\partial\phi/\partial x^i$ is naturally covariant. An infinitesimal displacement $dx^i$ is naturally contravariant.
$$ \mathbf{b}^i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j = \delta^i_j \quad\text{(reciprocal bases)} $$
Coordinate Transformation
Changing from $x^i$ to $x^{i'}$ uses the Jacobian matrix and its inverse. The determinant gives the local volume scaling factor.
$$ J^i_{\;j} = \frac{\partial x^{i'}}{\partial x^j}, \qquad V^{i'} = \frac{\partial x^{i'}}{\partial x^k} V^k $$
The visualization shows Cartesian and polar planes side-by-side. The Jacobian for polar coordinates is displayed in the 3D view.
$$ x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta $$ $$ r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\quad \theta = \arctan\frac{y}{x} $$
$$ J = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\frac{\sin\theta}{r} & \frac{\cos\theta}{r} \end{bmatrix} $$
Contravariant, Covariant & Mixed Tensors
A tensor of type $(p,q)$ has $p$ contravariant and $q$ covariant indices. Each index transforms with the appropriate Jacobian factor.
TypeNotationExample
$(0,0)$Scalar$\phi$, $T$
$(1,0)$Contravariant vector$dx^i$
$(0,1)$Covariant vector$\partial_i\phi$
$(1,1)$Mixed tensor$\delta^i_j$
$(0,2)$Covariant 2-tensor$g_{ij}$
$(2,0)$Contravariant 2-tensor$g^{ij}$
$$ T'^{i'_1\ldots i'_p}_{\;\;j'_1\ldots j'_q} = \frac{\partial x^{i'_1}}{\partial x^{k_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{l_1}}{\partial x^{j'_1}}\cdots T^{k_1\ldots k_p}_{\;\;l_1\ldots l_q} $$
The metric tensor raises and lowers indices: $V^i = g^{ij}V_j$ and $V_i = g_{ij}V^j$.
The Kronecker Delta
$\delta^i_j$ is the identity tensor. It is invariant under all coordinate changes and serves as the index-replacement operator.
$$ \delta^i_j = \begin{cases} 1 & i=j \\ 0 & i\neq j \end{cases} $$
$$ \delta^i_i = n \quad\text{(dimension)} $$ $$ \delta^i_j V^j = V^i \quad\text{(index replacement)} $$ $$ \delta^i_j = \frac{\partial x^i}{\partial x^j} \quad\text{(chain rule identity)} $$
The 3D view shows the $3\times3$ identity matrix. Unlike other tensors whose components mix under transformation, $\delta^i_j$ remains the identity in every basis.
