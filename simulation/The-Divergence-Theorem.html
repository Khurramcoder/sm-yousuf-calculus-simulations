🌊 The Divergence Theorem

1. Physical Intuition

The Divergence Theorem connects two fundamental concepts in vector calculus: flux through a surface and divergence within a volume.

Fluid Flow Model

Imagine a region of space filled with a moving fluid. The vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z)$ represents the velocity of the fluid at each point.

Sources and Sinks:
  • Source ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} > 0$): Fluid is being created at this point (like a spring or fountain). Net flow is outward.
  • Sink ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} < 0$): Fluid is being destroyed at this point (like a drain). Net flow is inward.
  • Incompressible ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0$): No fluid is created or destroyed. Flow through any closed surface equals zero.

Surface Flux vs Volume Divergence

Surface Flux ($\oiint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS$): Measures the net rate at which fluid flows outward through the boundary surface $S$. Think of it as "how much water is leaving the region per second."

Volume Divergence ($\iiint_V \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \, dV$): Measures the total rate of fluid creation/destruction inside the volume $V$. Sum up all sources minus sinks.

Key Insight: The total fluid leaving a closed region must equal the total fluid created inside that region (minus what was destroyed). This is conservation of mass!

2. Mathematical Statement

The Divergence Theorem states:

$$\iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV = \oiint_{\partial V} (\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dS$$

Notation Explained

  • $V$: A bounded region (volume) in 3D space
  • $\partial V$ or $S$: The closed boundary surface of $V$
  • $\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$: A continuously differentiable vector field
  • $\mathbf{n}$: The outward-pointing unit normal vector to the surface
  • $dS$: Differential surface area element
  • $dV$: Differential volume element ($dx \, dy \, dz$)
  • $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}$: The divergence of $\mathbf{F}$
Important: The surface must be closed and oriented with outward normals for the theorem to hold.

3. Proof by Cancellation

We prove the theorem by dividing the volume into infinitesimal cubes and showing that internal fluxes cancel.

Step 1: Infinitesimal Cube

Consider a small cube with dimensions $dx \times dy \times dz$ centered at $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$. The divergence at the center is:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}$$

The net outward flux through this cube is approximately:

$$(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\right) dx \, dy \, dz$$

Step 2: Flux Through Cube Faces

For the face at $x = x_0 + dx/2$ (right face, normal $\mathbf{n} = \hat{\mathbf{i}}$):

$$\text{Flux}_{\text{right}} \approx P\left(x_0 + \frac{dx}{2}, y_0, z_0\right) \, dy \, dz$$

For the face at $x = x_0 - dx/2$ (left face, normal $\mathbf{n} = -\hat{\mathbf{i}}$):

$$\text{Flux}_{\text{left}} \approx -P\left(x_0 - \frac{dx}{2}, y_0, z_0\right) \, dy \, dz$$

Net flux through $x$-faces:

$$\left[P\left(x_0 + \frac{dx}{2}\right) - P\left(x_0 - \frac{dx}{2}\right)\right] dy \, dz \approx \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} dx \, dy \, dz$$

Similar analysis applies to $y$ and $z$ faces.

Step 3: Cancellation of Internal Fluxes

When we tile the volume $V$ with many small cubes, adjacent cubes share faces. The flux through a shared face appears with opposite signs for the two cubes (outward normals point in opposite directions).

Result: All internal fluxes cancel! Only flux through the outer boundary $\partial V$ remains.

Step 4: Summing Over Volume

Sum the divergence over all small cubes:

$$\sum_i (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})_i \, \Delta V_i \approx \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV$$

This equals the sum of net fluxes from each cube, which equals the net flux through the outer boundary:

$$\iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV = \oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS \quad \blacksquare$$

4. Worked Example

Problem

Verify the Divergence Theorem for:

  • Vector field: $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x, y, z \rangle$
  • Surface: Unit sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \leq 1$

Method A: Volume Integral (Divergence Side)

Step 1: Compute divergence

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$$

Step 2: Integrate over the unit sphere volume

$$\iiint_V 3 \, dV = 3 \cdot \text{Volume of unit sphere} = 3 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi(1)^3 = 4\pi$$

Result: $\iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV = 4\pi$

Method B: Surface Integral (Flux Side)

Step 1: Parametrize the unit sphere

$$\mathbf{r}(\theta, \phi) = \langle \sin\phi\cos\theta, \sin\phi\sin\theta, \cos\phi \rangle$$

where $\theta \in [0, 2\pi]$, $\phi \in [0, \pi]$

Step 2: Compute outward unit normal

For a sphere of radius $R$, the outward normal is:

$$\mathbf{n} = \frac{\langle x, y, z \rangle}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}} = \langle x, y, z \rangle \quad \text{(on unit sphere)}$$

Step 3: Compute $\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}$

$$\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} = \langle x, y, z \rangle \cdot \langle x, y, z \rangle = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$$

Step 4: Surface integral

$$\oiint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \oiint_S 1 \, dS = \text{Surface area of unit sphere} = 4\pi(1)^2 = 4\pi$$

Result: $\oiint_S (\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dS = 4\pi$

✓ Verification Complete

Both methods yield $4\pi$. The Divergence Theorem holds!

5. Applications

Fluid Dynamics

Conservation of mass equation (continuity equation):

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0$$

Electromagnetism

Gauss's Law for electricity:

$$\oiint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}$$

Heat Transfer

Conservation of energy in heat conduction.
